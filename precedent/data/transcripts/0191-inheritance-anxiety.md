# Session 0191 — windfall, and the distress is about the windfall

> Fictional. No real advisor, client, firm, or account.
>
> Included because distress is not only a downside phenomenon. A precedent library built
> only from losses would teach a coach that anxiety and falling balances are the same event.

[t01] **CLI:** My mother died in January and there's money, and I've done nothing with it for two months and I feel awful about that.

[t02] **ADV:** I'm very sorry about your mother. And doing nothing for two months with money that arrived that way isn't a failure — for a lot of people it's the correct speed.

[t03] **CLI:** Everyone keeps telling me I'm losing ground by leaving it in cash.

[t04] **ADV:** People do say that. They're arithmetically correct and they're missing the point by a mile.

[t05] **CLI:** So what should I do with it?

[t06] **ADV:** Before that — can I ask what the money feels like? Because I've noticed the answer to that predicts what goes wrong later far better than any risk questionnaire I've got.

[t07] **CLI:** …It feels like it isn't mine. Like spending it would be admitting she's gone.

[t08] **ADV:** Thank you for saying that. That's the actual thing, and no allocation I recommend is going to work if we don't account for it.

[t09] **CLI:** It sounds stupid out loud.

[t10] **ADV:** It sounds like grief, which is not stupid, and it's also the single most common thing I hear in this conversation. You are not unusual and you are not being irrational.

[t11] **CLI:** So is leaving it in cash the wrong answer?

[t12] **ADV:** Let me give you both halves. Leaving it costs you growth, and over a decade that's a genuinely large number — I won't pretend otherwise. What it buys you is time to stop feeling like every decision is a referendum on your mother, and decisions made in that state are usually the expensive ones.

[t13] **CLI:** So there's a cost either way.

[t14] **ADV:** There's always a cost either way. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

[t15] **CLI:** What would you do?

[t16] **ADV:** My recommendation is that we do it in thirds over eighteen months rather than all at once, and I'll put the reasoning in writing. But I want to say something else first, which is that you might want to talk to someone who isn't a financial advisor about the part where it doesn't feel like yours. We work with a counsellor who does exactly this — grief and inherited money. I can introduce you, and it doesn't replace these conversations, it runs alongside them.

[t17] **CLI:** I think I'd like that.

[t18] **ADV:** I'll make the introduction this week. So: the money stays where it is for now, we've got a thirds-over-eighteen-months plan to look at when you're ready, I'm introducing you to Rachel, and you have no deadline from me. None of this is urgent and I don't want you to feel chased.

[t19] **CLI:** That's the first time in two months I've felt less awful about it.

[t20] **ADV:** Then that was the useful part of the call, not the allocation. Nothing moves until you say so.
